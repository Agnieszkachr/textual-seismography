Textual Seismography

Detecting redactional seams in the Book of Isaiah with dual-model transformer perplexity.

Agnieszka Blanka Ziemińska
Pontifical University of John Paul II, Kraków
Centre for Digital Humanities, Institute of Literary Research, Polish Academy of Sciences, Warsaw

A seismologist does not dig for the fault line. She stays on the surface, puts down an instrument, and records where the ground behaves differently from the ground on either side.

This project does the same with a text. Not who wrote this, but where does the surface break — and it puts the question to two neural language models of deliberately different architectures, which answer independently and are believed only when they agree.

Two instruments

1

GPT-Neo — surprise

A causal model, fine-tuned on Hebrew, reading forwards only. Given a sliding window of three preceding verses it reports how badly the next verse is predicted. The loss is masked so that only the target verse counts. A reader stumbling.

2

DictaBERT — strangeness

A masked model that sees the whole verse at once. Every token is hidden in turn and restored; the mean log-probability gives a pseudo-perplexity. It measures whether the verse is odd in itself. A proof-reader frowning.

The rule of two witnesses. Both signals become rolling Z-scores over a centred window of five verses, so each verse is judged against its immediate neighbourhood. A verse is flagged only when both models independently reach Z ≥ 1.9 on it. If one objects alone, the reading is thrown away as an artefact of that architecture. Deliberately conservative: precision over recall.

What it found in Isaiah

  • Twenty-two shared seams — verses where both models independently cross the threshold. Chance alone would give about 7.5 of them, or about 10 once verse length is held constant; the observed count is not reached in 20,000 draws of either null.
  • Chapter 53 is the most anomalous chapter in the book (mean CFI +0.37), and Isa 53:8 is one of the 22 shared seams — two networks that know nothing of Jewish or Christian interpretation marking the edges of the Fourth Servant Song.
  • Two of the six highest single-verse scores in the whole book fall in chapter 28, which is not usually treated as a primary compositional boundary.
  • The anti-idol material at Isa 40:19 and 46:5 registers as stylistically alien to its surroundings — a measurable argument for treating the satires as self-contained insertions.
  • Chapter 40, where the tradition places the main seam, is a caution rather than a result. The step is there — chapter 39 at −0.18, chapter 40 at +0.11 — but it is the 13th largest of the 65 chapter boundaries, and the level does not shift across it: chapters 30–39 average +0.00 against +0.01 for 40–49. The instrument locates verses, not regimes.

The instrument dates nothing. It locates boundaries in the text as we now have it; every historical conclusion beyond that point is the philologist's work.

Where the numbers come from

The scores are isaiah_gpt.scores.json and isaiah_dicta.scores.json, produced on 23 February 2026 and included in the repository. Each record carries its own perplexity_score and z_score; adding the two Z-score columns gives the Combined Fracture Index directly. A later revision of the pipeline computes a different, stricter statistic — global Z-scores with FDR correction — which is kept in the repository but is not what the paper reports. The README sets out the difference in full.
